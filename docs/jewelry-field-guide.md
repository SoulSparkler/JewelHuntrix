1. How to use this guide

Three jobs, one document. This reference does triple duty. Read it front to back to train your eye; jump to the cheat tables when you are mid-hunt on Vinted, at a vide-grenier, or at a brocante; and lift the structured signals straight into a scanner ruleset when you want a tool to flag candidates for you.

The golden rule of attribution. A mark tells you what something claims to be. Construction tells you what it actually is. The most valuable skill is reading an unmarked piece from the back, because that is where most of the money hides and where the fewest sellers look.

The five-second flip

Whenever you pick up a piece, turn it over first. The back reveals more than the front:

1. Mark? Signature, copyright symbol, sterling stamp, hallmark, or nothing.

2. Construction. Soldered or glued stones? Prong-set or pasted? Riveted? Hand-finished or stamped?

3. Wear pattern. Age-appropriate patina and honest wear, or artificially aged / suspiciously bright?

4. Findings. Clasp type, pin assembly, earring backs date a piece more reliably than style.

5. Weight & feel. Quality costume jewelry is solid and cool to the touch. Lightweight and warm suggests cheap pot metal or plastic.

2. Marked vs unmarked: what a signature does and does not tell you

2.1 Why so much is unmarked

A huge share of valuable vintage costume jewelry was never permanently signed. Many makers identified pieces only with a paper hang tag or a backing card that was thrown away the moment the piece was worn. DeLizza & Elster (the firm behind “Juliana”) is the textbook case: their jewelry is essentially never stamped, and the famous “Juliana” hang tag was only used for roughly one year starting in 1967. Eisenberg moved to paper tags from the late 1950s into the 1970s, leaving many genuine pieces unmarked. So “no mark” is not the same as “no value.”

2.2 Reading a mark

When a mark is present, read it for three things: the name, the dating clues around it, and whether it looks right.

Copyright symbol ©. In US costume jewelry the © generally indicates a piece made after 1955, when design copyright became common. A name with no © is often (not always) earlier.

“Pat. Pend.” or a patent number. Datable to a specific filing window; a number can often be looked up to a year.

“Sterling”. Wartime and 1940s pieces (roughly 1942–1948 for several makers) used sterling silver because base pot metal was restricted. A sterling-marked costume piece usually signals 1940s manufacture and higher intrinsic value.

Mark style and placement. The same maker changed logos over decades. Script versus block lettering, crown shapes, and whether the mark is cast into the mold or applied as a separate plate all narrow the date and flag fakes.

Cast-in vs applied plate — a fast fake tell

Older, genuine pieces from makers like Eisenberg usually had the mark embedded directly into the mold, often with a tiny manufacturing code (a single initial or number).

Applied oval/rectangular nameplates soldered on afterward are a common sign of a reproduction, especially on “Eisenberg” and “Trifari” pieces. Originals were typically marked in the mold.

3.2 Foiled vs unfoiled (open-back) stones

Foil-backed rhinestones have a mirror coating on the back to boost sparkle and are usually closed in. Open-back / unfoiled stones let light pass straight through like a real gemstone and are a sign of a more confident, higher-quality maker. When you can see daylight through the stones from the back, take a closer look.

3.3 Findings as a date stamp

Brooch pin assembly. A C-clasp (simple hook) points to early/antique; a trombone (cylinder) clasp suggests European or earlier 20th c.; a modern roll-over safety catch is mid-century onward. A pin stem extending past the body edge is an early sign.

Earring backs. Screw-backs predate clip-backs; clip-backs predate posts for pierced ears (which became dominant after the late 1960s). Posts on an otherwise “1940s” piece are a warning.

Clasps on necklaces/bracelets. Fold-over and box clasps, spring rings, and the weight of the metal all help date and grade a piece.

4. Case study: “What is a Juliana brooch?”

Short answer. “Juliana” is a collector nickname for unsigned jewelry made by DeLizza & Elster (D&E), a New York firm founded in the late 1940s that manufactured for hundreds of other labels. The name comes from a hang tag the company used for only about a year starting in 1967 — “Juliana” was reportedly the name of William DeLizza’s mother. Because the jewelry itself was never permanently signed, it can only be attributed by construction and design.

Why it matters for sourcing. Sellers constantly list these as plain “vintage rhinestone” pieces with no idea what they are, while correctly identified D&E sets command real money. This is exactly the gap an unmarked-attribution eye exploits. Equally, the term is wildly overused — plenty of listings say “Juliana style” for pieces that are not D&E at all, which is its own opportunity (mispriced upward, avoid) and risk.

4.1 The D&E identification stack

No single trait proves Juliana. Look for several of these together, and always weigh the whole piece:

Five-link and band bracelet construction. The most reliable starting point. On the back, bracelets show five links alternating with connector bands. Links range rectangular to oval; bands can be ribbed or textured. Once you can spot this bracelet, you can match brooches, earrings and necklaces to it as a set.

Figure-eight puddling. On the reverse, round stone cups were soldered together while the piece was built, leaving a distinctive figure-eight pattern of “puddled” solder. A genuine D&E tell.

Sparse, heavy rivets. D&E used a specific sturdy rivet sparingly. Beware: pieces with dozens of rivets and “swedged” construction are usually Beau Jewels or Judy Lee, not D&E.

Soldered-on pin assembly. On brooches the joint-and-catch is soldered directly to the body, not applied as a separate riveted unit. Figure-eight puddling plus a riveted pin assembly usually means NOT Juliana.

Open-backed elongated navettes. Heavy use of elongated marquis (navette) rhinestones, often unfoiled and set in open-backed cups.

Distinctive specialty stones. “Easter egg” (stippled) cabochons, “watermelon” stones mimicking watermelon tourmaline, rivoli stones, margarita stones, art-glass and dangling bead accents.

Attribution discipline (don't get burned)

Never call something “Juliana” unless multiple D&E traits agree. Other makers used paper tags and similar stones, so one feature alone is not proof.

Cross-reference before you buy or list high. Use the “Is It Juliana?” database and the D&E study communities, and check sold (not asking) comps on WorthPoint for the actual realized price.

Colour drives price. Vivid blue, red, fuchsia and watermelon command the most; clear-crystal and brown/black “black diamond” pieces are more affordable and more common.

5.3 European & couture (handle with care, big upside)

Chanel. Authentic vintage (not reproductions) commands top dollar. Early-1990s-onward pieces may be marked “Histoire de Verre.” Authentication is its own discipline — verify before paying couture prices.

Christian Dior. Early Dior costume pieces (often made under licence by Mitchel Maer or Henkel & Grosse, sometimes date-stamped) are highly collectible.

Gripoix. Hand-poured glass (pâte de verre) made for Chanel, Dior and others; the poured-glass look itself is the value signal.

Schiaparelli. Did not sign before 1930; signed pieces with bold surrealist stones and “watermelon”/iridescent glass are prized.

Joseff of Hollywood. Pieces with film provenance attract serious money; distinctive matte “Russian gold” finish.

French market specifics

Poçon (hallmark) culture is strong in France. The eagle’s head (tête d’aigle) guarantees 18k gold; the boar’s head (hure de sanglier) and crab mark guarantee silver. A maker’s mark in a lozenge sits alongside.

Vide-greniers and brocantes routinely surface unrecognised hallmarked silver and gold mixed in with costume lots. Carry a loupe and a magnet.

8. Spotting reproductions and forged marks

Repro houses cast new pieces from vintage molds and stamp them with valuable names. The common tells:

Oversize. New pieces are often noticeably larger than originals because they were cast in new, bigger molds. Check measurements against a reference book or sold comps.

Heavy-handed finish. Thick vermeil/gilt, flat monotone colour with no soft shading, irregular facets, or plastic where glass should be.

Applied nameplate instead of cast-in mark. Especially on “Eisenberg” and “Trifari.” Originals were usually marked in the mold.

Lone “Trifari” with nothing else. Genuine Trifari rarely appears as the bare word — expect a crown, a ©, or patent wording alongside.

Anachronistic findings. Modern posts/clasps on a piece claiming to be 1940s.

Verdict discipline: one odd feature is not proof of a fake — makers varied across eras. Stack the evidence; require several signals to agree before you call it.

These convert the guide into machine-usable logic: hard gates, positive flags, and red flags. Treat them as scoring inputs, not absolutes — the same discipline of “require several to agree” applies.

Hard gates (must-have to surface, tune per use)

Precious-metal stamp present (925 / 14k / 18k / 750 / plat) — route to a metal-value branch regardless of design.

Any top-tier maker name in title or description — surface even at low price.

Photo shows the BACK of the piece — unmarked-attribution opportunities live here; deprioritise listings with no reverse shot for high-value flips.

Red flags (lower score / flag for manual review)

“Style” / “inspired by” / “in the manner of” next to a maker name = not attributed.

“Trifari” with no crown/©/patent language; “Eisenberg” on an applied plate; oversized measurements vs known originals = possible fake.

Glued stones, plastic stones, heavy brassing, posts on a claimed-vintage piece.

Always confirm value against sold/realized comps (WorthPoint, completed listings), never asking prices, before committing.
